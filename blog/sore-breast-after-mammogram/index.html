Is It Normal for Your Breast to Be Sore or Bruised After a Mammogram?

November 12, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, mild soreness after a mammogram is common, and a small bruise can happen. Most pain fades fast: a 2003 study in the Journal of Pain and Symptom Management found it ended within 10 minutes for 67% of women who had pain. Call your doctor if pain gets worse, a lump appears, or an implant changes shape.

How common is soreness after a mammogram?

Very common. A mammogram works by pressing the breast flat between two plates. RadiologyInfo, run by the American College of Radiology and RSNA, says compression gives a clearer picture and lowers the X-ray dose. It also says women with sensitive breasts may feel discomfort. MedlinePlus describes "some brief pain or discomfort" when the breast is pressed down.

Several large surveys show how often this happens:

  • In a Dutch screening program, 72.9% of 945 women described the mammogram as mildly to severely painful.
  • In San Diego, 52% of 1,800 women reported moderate to extreme discomfort when asked about 3 weeks after screening.
  • A Duke study found that up to 91% of women reported some pain, but fewer than 15% reported intense pain.

So a tender feeling on the drive home is normal. It does not mean the test went wrong. If you want to lower the pain at your next visit, see our tips for a less painful mammogram. This article covers what happens after the exam.

How long does the pain usually last?

For most women, minutes. A survey of 399 women in Jerusalem, published in 2003, asked about the length of the pain. Of the women who had pain, 67% said it was gone within 10 minutes. That leaves about a third whose pain lasted longer than 10 minutes. The abstract does not say how much longer.

We did not find a large study that counts how many women are still sore days later. So be careful with any website that gives you an exact number of days. What the evidence does support is this: the pain is usually short, and it should get better each day, not worse.

Some women are more likely to hurt. In the Dutch study, pain was more common in women with sensitive breasts, breast tenderness in the 3 days before the exam, anxiety, or pain at an earlier mammogram. The Duke study linked more pain to dense breasts and to pain at the last mammogram.

Why do some people bruise, and who is more prone?

A bruise forms when small blood vessels under the skin break and leak. Firm pressure on the breast can do this. We found no study that counts how often a routine mammogram leaves a bruise. Some people bruise more easily from any pressure.

  • People on blood thinners. MedlinePlus says you are likely to bruise more easily if you take a blood thinner such as aspirin, warfarin, or clopidogrel. Do not stop these medicines before a mammogram unless your doctor tells you to.
  • People with breast implants. The exam adds extra pictures called implant displacement views, so the breast gets more handling. A review of FDA reports found that pain made up 29.3% of mammography-related implant events, and swelling made up 7.3%. These events were rare overall.
  • People who already had tender breasts. Tissue that is sore before the exam often stays sore after it.

A bruise on the breast changes color like a bruise anywhere else. Our guide to mammograms with breast implants covers how to book and what to tell the technologist.

What should you expect day by day after a mammogram?

This timeline is built from the sources in this article. Your own recovery can be faster or slower.

  1. During and right after the exam: Brief pain or pressure is expected (MedlinePlus). For 67% of women with pain, it ends within 10 minutes (Sapir, 2003).
  2. The rest of day 1: Some tenderness can stay. If a spot hurts, wrap ice in a towel and hold it there for up to 15 minutes at a time, once per hour (MedlinePlus). A supportive bra, such as a sports bra, can help (MedlinePlus).
  3. Days 1 to 3: If a bruise forms, it often starts reddish and then turns bluish-purple (MedlinePlus). Soreness should ease, not grow. With saline implants, note that saline from a rupture can leak right away or over several days (FDA).
  4. Days 4 to 10: A bruise turns greenish-yellow as it fades. Tenderness from compression alone should be gone or close to it.
  5. About 2 weeks: Most bruises are gone by about two weeks, though some take longer to fade (MedlinePlus).
  6. Past 2 weeks: Pain that persists with no clear reason is a reason to call your provider (MedlinePlus).

When is pain or bruising a reason to call?

Most soreness needs no care at all. Call your doctor or the imaging center if you notice any of these:

  • Pain that gets worse instead of better, or that lasts with no clear reason.
  • Swelling that grows, or a feeling of strong pressure in the area. MedlinePlus says to get care right away for extreme pressure in a bruised area.
  • A new lump. MedlinePlus lists a new lump that does not go away after your period as a reason to call.
  • Redness, pus, or fever, which can be signs of infection.
  • Nipple discharge that is bloody or clear.
  • Bruises that appear with no injury, or many bruises at once.

Do not assume that a lump is "just from the mammogram." A mammogram does not cause cancer, but new symptoms still need a check. If your results come back and you are asked to return, our callback guide explains the next steps. For a general symptom checker, see symptom.md.

What if you have breast implants?

Implant rupture from a mammogram is rare. The FDA lists "compression during a mammogram" as one possible cause of rupture. But a 2022 review of the FDA's adverse event database found only 41 mammography-related reports between 2008 and November 2018, out of 20,539 implant reports. The authors called the risk extremely low and said it should not keep women from screening. An earlier FDA review found 66 such reports in its database.

Know the warning signs, per the FDA:

  • Saline implants: the breast loses its size or shape. The leak can happen at once or over several days.
  • Silicone implants: a smaller breast, hard lumps over the implant or chest, pain or tenderness, or numbness or tingling. Many silicone ruptures cause no symptoms at all.
  • Hardening: a breast that turns firm, painful, or abnormal in shape can be capsular contracture.

If you notice a change in implant shape or size after your mammogram, call your surgeon or doctor. The FDA says MRI is the most effective way to find a silicone rupture. You can learn more about that test at mri.md.

The bottom line

Soreness after a mammogram is normal and usually short. Most women who have pain say it is gone within minutes. A small bruise can happen, especially if you take a blood thinner, and should fade over about two weeks. Use ice and a supportive bra for comfort. Call your doctor for pain that gets worse, growing swelling, a new lump, or any change in an implant. Pain is not a reason to skip your next screening.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I take a pain reliever for soreness after a mammogram?

MedlinePlus lists acetaminophen or ibuprofen as home care for breast pain. Follow the dose on the label. If you take a blood thinner, ask your doctor first, because some pain relievers can raise bleeding risk.

Should I use heat or ice on a sore breast after a mammogram?

MedlinePlus lists either heat or ice for breast pain. For a bruise, it advises ice wrapped in a towel for up to 15 minutes at a time, once per hour. Do not put ice right on the skin.

Can a mammogram cause a lump?

Compression can cause a bruise, which may feel tender or a little firm. A new lump that does not go away should be checked, and you should not assume the mammogram caused it. MedlinePlus lists a new lump that lasts past your period as a reason to call your provider.

Will the soreness be worse at my next mammogram?

Not necessarily, but studies show that pain at an earlier mammogram predicts pain at the next one. Tell the technologist about your last visit. They can adjust compression, and RadiologyInfo says they will use less if discomfort is significant.

Should I stop my blood thinner before a mammogram to avoid bruising?

No, not on your own. A routine screening mammogram does not usually need any change in medicines. Ask the doctor who prescribed the blood thinner if you have concerns.
