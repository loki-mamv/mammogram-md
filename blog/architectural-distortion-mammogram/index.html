What Does Architectural Distortion Mean on a Mammogram?

The short answer: Architectural distortion means part of the breast tissue looks pulled, puckered, or bent toward one point, with no clear lump. Scars, benign radial scars, and cancer can all cause it. A 2021 AJR meta-analysis found 34.6% of unexplained distortions on 3D mammograms were cancer, so a biopsy is the usual next step.

What is architectural distortion on a mammogram?

Normal breast tissue has a soft, fan-like pattern. Thin strands of tissue run toward the nipple in smooth lines. Architectural distortion is a break in that pattern.

Radiologists use a shared vocabulary called the BI-RADS lexicon. In that lexicon, distortion means the normal architecture is disturbed without a visible mass. A 2024 review in Gland Surgery sums up the lexicon wording: it often looks like thin lines radiating from a point, or like tissue pulled in at its edge.

A useful picture is a bedsheet pinched in the middle. You do not see the fingers. You only see the wrinkles pointing toward them.

This is a different finding from the ones in our other guides. An asymmetry is a patch of tissue that looks denser on one side than the other. Calcifications are tiny white calcium specks. Distortion is about the shape of the tissue, not extra density or specks. It is also less common. A 2015 Duke study in AJR found suspicious distortion in 435 of 231,051 mammograms, or 0.2%.

What can cause architectural distortion besides cancer?

Many things can pull breast tissue out of line. The 2024 Gland Surgery review lists both benign and malignant causes:

  • Past surgery or biopsy. Scar tissue from a lumpectomy, reduction, or needle biopsy can pull nearby tissue. Even a cyst drained with a needle can leave a distortion.
  • Radial scar or complex sclerosing lesion. This is a benign growth that looks like a star on imaging. In the Duke study, it was the most common benign result, at 7.3% of biopsies.
  • Adenosis. This is a benign overgrowth of the milk-making glands.
  • Invasive breast cancer. Cancer can pull on nearby tissue and show up as distortion before a clear mass is visible.
  • Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS). This early, non-invasive cancer was the sole result in only 4.1% of cases in the Duke study.

Tell the imaging center about any breast surgery, biopsy, or injury. If a distortion lines up with a known scar, the radiologist may call it benign and skip further tests. The concern is distortion with no known cause.

How often is architectural distortion cancer?

The answer depends a lot on how the distortion was found. Published rates range from about 10% to about 75%. Here is what the studies we read report:

  • Older 2D mammograms, Duke, 2004 to 2013: 74.5% of suspicious distortions were cancer (275 of 369), per the 2015 AJR study. The rate was 67.0% on screening exams and 83.1% on diagnostic exams.
  • 3D mammograms, pooled data: 34.6% were cancer across 13 studies and 857 distortions, per the 2021 AJR meta-analysis. Another 27.4% were benign and 32.9% were high-risk lesions (not cancer, but needing more follow-up).
  • Seen only on 3D: 37% were cancer across 14 groups and 1,361 lesions, per a 2025 meta-analysis in Academic Radiology.
  • Seen only on 3D, one center: 10.2% were cancer, compared with 43.4% for distortion visible on 2D, per a 2018 University of Virginia study in Radiology.

Two features raise the odds. The first is a matching spot on ultrasound. In the Duke study, distortion with an ultrasound match was cancer 82.9% of the time, versus 27.9% without one. The second is seeing the distortion on both standard views. A 2021 Journal of Breast Imaging study found one-view findings were less often cancer.

These are group averages from biopsied cases. They are not your personal risk. Your radiologist sees your images and your history.

Why does 3D mammography find more distortions?

A 2D mammogram flattens the breast into one image. Overlapping tissue can hide a subtle pull. 3D mammography (tomosynthesis) takes thin slices, so the radiologist can scroll through the tissue layer by layer.

A 2016 RadioGraphics review from Yale notes that distortion is found more often on tomosynthesis and may not show on 2D at all. In the Virginia study, 48.8% of distortions in women who had both tests were seen only on 3D.

This cuts both ways. 3D finds more early cancers. It also finds more benign radial scars and small scars. That is why the cancer rate per distortion is lower on 3D than in older 2D studies. Even so, every study above still judged the rate high enough to recommend a biopsy.

What happens after a radiologist sees distortion?

If the finding came from a screening mammogram, you will get a callback first. Our mammogram callback guide covers that visit. The usual steps look like this:

  1. Screening result of BI-RADS 0. The American Cancer Society describes this as incomplete. You need more imaging.
  2. Diagnostic mammogram. Spot compression, magnified views, or extra 3D slices confirm the distortion is real. Some apparent distortions are just overlapping tissue and go away.
  3. Targeted ultrasound. The radiologist looks for a matching spot. The 2021 AJR meta-analysis found 78.3% of cancers had one. A missing match does not rule out cancer.
  4. Final BI-RADS category. A real, unexplained distortion is usually rated BI-RADS 4 or 5. ACS lists the cancer likelihood for 4A as 2% to 10%, 4B as 10% to 50%, and 4C as 50% to 95%.
  5. Biopsy. If ultrasound shows a spot, the biopsy is usually ultrasound-guided. If not, it may be done with stereotactic or 3D guidance. Our breast biopsy guide explains the procedure.
  6. Pathology and next plan. Cancer leads to a surgical consult. A radial scar or other high-risk result may lead to a surgical consult or follow-up imaging. A clearly benign result that matches the images usually returns you to routine screening.

Breast MRI sometimes helps when the distortion cannot be seen on ultrasound. In a 2020 Toronto study in European Radiology, none of 106 women with distortion and a negative MRI had cancer. MRI also flagged many benign radial scars, so a positive MRI is far from proof. For how the MRI test itself works, see mri.md.

What should you ask your care team?

  • Is the distortion near a past surgery, biopsy, or drained cyst?
  • Is it seen on one view or both views?
  • Did the ultrasound show a matching spot?
  • What BI-RADS category, and which subgroup (4A, 4B, or 4C)?
  • Which type of biopsy do you plan, and why?
  • If the result is a radial scar, will I need surgery or follow-up imaging?

The bottom line

Architectural distortion means the breast tissue looks pulled toward a point with no clear mass. It has benign causes, such as scars and radial scars. It also carries a real cancer risk, about one in three on 3D mammograms in pooled studies. Because imaging alone cannot tell them apart, a biopsy is the standard next step. Most people with this finding go through the same path: extra views, ultrasound, then a needle biopsy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can architectural distortion go away on its own?

Sometimes an apparent distortion on a screening mammogram is only overlapping tissue. It disappears on spot compression views at the callback visit. A true, persistent distortion with no known cause does not usually get watched; radiologists recommend a biopsy instead.

Is architectural distortion the same as a radial scar?

No. Architectural distortion is what the radiologist sees on the image. A radial scar is one possible tissue diagnosis behind it, found only by biopsy. Radial scars are not cancer.

Does architectural distortion always mean I need a biopsy?

Not always. If the distortion lines up with a known surgical or biopsy scar, the radiologist may call it benign. If there is no known cause, published studies support a biopsy because cancer rates are well above the 2% threshold for BI-RADS 4.

Can architectural distortion be seen on ultrasound?

Often, but not always. In the 2021 AJR meta-analysis, 78.3% of cancers that showed as distortion on 3D mammograms had a matching ultrasound finding. When ultrasound shows nothing, the biopsy can still be done with stereotactic, 3D, or MRI guidance.

How long does it take to get biopsy results for architectural distortion?

Timing varies by center. Ask the imaging team who will call you and when. If the result does not match the images, the radiologist may recommend a second biopsy or surgical removal.
