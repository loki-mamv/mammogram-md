Enlarged Lymph Node on a Mammogram: What Does It Mean?

The short answer: An enlarged lymph node on a mammogram usually means the node is reacting to something, such as a recent vaccine, an infection, or an inflammatory illness. Cancer is a less common cause. Under the Society of Breast Imaging's February 2022 guidance, a swollen armpit node on the same side as a recent vaccine can be called benign.

What is a lymph node finding on a mammogram?

Lymph nodes are small, bean-shaped parts of the immune system. They filter fluid and help the body fight germs. MedlinePlus notes that the medical name for swollen nodes is lymphadenopathy. You may also see the word adenopathy on a report.

A mammogram can show two kinds of nodes:

  • Axillary nodes. These sit in the armpit. The edge of a mammogram image includes part of the armpit, so the radiologist often sees some of these nodes.
  • Intramammary nodes. These sit inside the breast tissue itself. A 2019 review in the British Journal of Radiology calls them one of the most common benign findings at screening mammography.

Seeing a node is normal. The question is whether the node looks normal. The American College of Radiology (ACR) BI-RADS mammography lexicon (v2025 form) describes a normal node as a smooth, kidney-shaped mass with fat in its center (the "hilum"). Normal intramammary nodes are generally 1 cm or smaller, though some larger nodes still count as normal.

What makes a radiologist call a node abnormal?

Radiologists judge a node mostly by shape, not by length. The ACR lexicon says an abnormal axillary node may look rounder, have a thicker outer layer (cortex), or lose its fatty center. For armpit nodes, it states that the degree of suspicion is independent of length.

The 2019 intramammary node review lists the same warning signs, plus two more:

  • A fatty center that is smaller or gone
  • A thickened cortex
  • Edges that are not smooth and sharp
  • A node that has grown or changed since your last mammogram

This is why your old mammograms matter. A node that has looked the same for years is reassuring. A node that is new or larger gets a closer look.

What causes an enlarged lymph node besides cancer?

Most swollen nodes are the immune system doing its job. MedlinePlus lists infections as the most common cause of swollen lymph nodes in general. Common benign causes that can show up near the breast include:

  • A recent vaccine in that arm. COVID-19 vaccines brought this to wide attention, but MedlinePlus notes that some other immunizations can also swell nodes.
  • An infection. Skin infections, colds, flu, and mononucleosis are on the MedlinePlus list.
  • Autoimmune disease. MedlinePlus names rheumatoid arthritis. HIV can also enlarge nodes.
  • Silicone. The 2019 review lists silicone-induced lymphadenopathy, which can occur with breast implants, as a benign cause.
  • Certain medicines. MedlinePlus names the seizure medicine phenytoin.

Cancer causes include breast cancer that has spread to the nodes, lymphoma, and leukemia. Swelling in both armpits can matter too. A 2012 Penn State study in The Breast Journal found bilateral swollen nodes with no known cause in only 12 of 74,926 screening mammograms. Five of those 12 women had non-Hodgkin lymphoma, so unexplained swelling on both sides needs a workup.

What if I had a vaccine recently?

Tell the technologist the date of the shot and which arm got it. That one detail can save you a callback.

The Society of Breast Imaging (SBI) statement, updated February 2022, is still the society's current guidance as of this writing. It makes these points for average-risk women with no cancer history:

  • Do not delay a screening mammogram around a COVID-19 vaccine.
  • Swelling in one armpit, on the same side as a recent shot, with nothing else suspicious, may be called BI-RADS 2 (benign).
  • Each practice sets its own definition of "recent." Published reports show swelling from 1 day to 71 days after the shot.
  • If a node is being watched, the follow-up interval should be 12 weeks or more.

European radiologists went further. The 2023 European Society of Breast Imaging revision calls same-side swelling within 12 weeks of a COVID-19 vaccine benign, with no further workup, in women without breast cancer history or suspicious findings.

The data back this up. A 2024 Massachusetts General Hospital study in the Journal of the American College of Radiology reviewed 44,473 screening mammograms. Same-side armpit swelling after vaccination appeared in 94 women (0.2%). Of the 77 rated benign at screening, none had cancer at 1-year follow-up. Our guide to COVID vaccines and mammogram results covers the vaccine data in more detail.

How is a BI-RADS category assigned for a lymph node?

The radiologist weighs how the node looks against your history. The ACR form ties each category to a plan:

  • BI-RADS 0 (incomplete). You need more imaging, often an ultrasound, before a final answer.
  • BI-RADS 2 (benign). A normal-looking node, or swelling with a clear benign cause such as a recent vaccine. Routine screening continues.
  • BI-RADS 3 (probably benign). The ACR puts the cancer risk at 2% or less. You return for a short-interval check, often 6 months.
  • BI-RADS 4 (suspicious). The node needs a tissue sample. The ACR range is above 2% to below 95%.

Our BI-RADS categories guide explains every category. A 2026 review in RadioGraphics reports that the next BI-RADS edition is expected to add a dedicated lymph node category across mammography, ultrasound, and MRI. Reports may change wording once it is published.

What happens next, step by step?

If your report mentions an enlarged or abnormal node, the path usually looks like this:

  1. Read the category. BI-RADS 1 or 2 means routine screening. BI-RADS 0, 3, or 4 means more steps.
  2. Share your history. Report recent vaccines, infections, arm injuries, implants, autoimmune disease, and any past cancer. A clear cause can end the workup.
  3. Get a diagnostic exam if called back. This is usually an ultrasound of the armpit or breast.
  4. Follow the plan for that result. A probably benign node gets a repeat check. For vaccine-related nodes, the SBI says to consider a BI-RADS 2 rating if a node has improved, more follow-up if unchanged, and sampling if it has grown.
  5. Have a needle biopsy if the node looks suspicious. MedlinePlus describes ultrasound-guided biopsy: numbing medicine, a tiny cut, and a fine or hollow needle guided to the spot. You do not need stitches. A small clip may mark the site.
  6. Review the result with your doctor. A benign result usually means routine care. A cancer result leads to a care plan, which may include breast MRI. mri.md explains MRI in plain language.

Our breast biopsy guide explains what the visit feels like.

Between visits, call your doctor if a node you can feel keeps growing, feels hard or fixed, or comes with fever, night sweats, or weight loss you cannot explain. MedlinePlus lists these as reasons to seek care.

The bottom line

Lymph nodes show up on many mammograms, and most are normal or reacting to something harmless. Shape and change over time matter more than size. Tell the imaging center about recent vaccines, infections, and implants. If a node is called suspicious, an ultrasound and a needle biopsy give a clear answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a lymph node in the breast normal?

Yes. Intramammary lymph nodes are one of the most common benign findings on screening mammograms. A normal one is smooth, kidney-shaped, and has a fatty center. The radiologist only worries if its shape changes or it grows.

Does a bigger lymph node mean cancer?

Not by itself. The ACR BI-RADS lexicon says suspicion for an armpit node does not depend on its length. Radiologists look at roundness, a thick outer layer, and loss of the fatty center instead.

Can a flu shot or other vaccine cause swollen nodes on a mammogram?

It can. MedlinePlus notes that some immunizations can swell lymph nodes, and COVID-19 vaccines are the best studied example on mammograms. Tell the technologist the date and arm of any recent shot so the radiologist can account for it.

Should I postpone my mammogram after a vaccine?

No. The Society of Breast Imaging said in February 2022 that it no longer recommends delaying screening mammograms around COVID-19 vaccines. The European Society of Breast Imaging said the same in 2023.

Will I need a biopsy for a swollen lymph node?

Only if the node looks suspicious and there is no clear benign cause. Many people need only an ultrasound or a follow-up exam. If a biopsy is needed, it is usually a needle biopsy guided by ultrasound, with no stitches.
