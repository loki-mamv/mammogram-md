Can Tattoos Affect Mammogram Results?

October 19, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Usually not, but sometimes yes. The best-documented problem is tattoo pigment that travels to the lymph nodes in the armpit, where it can look like tiny calcium deposits on a mammogram. That look-alike can lead to a callback or even a biopsy. The finding is benign, and telling the technologist about your tattoos helps the radiologist read it correctly.

Does tattoo ink show up on a mammogram?

Tattoo ink sits in the skin, and most of it stays there. Some pigment particles, though, are carried away by the lymphatic system and collect in nearby lymph nodes. Many tattoo inks contain metal-based pigments. An Ochsner Journal case report notes that heavy metals in tattoo ink can be deposited in axillary (armpit) lymph nodes and mimic malignant calcifications.

On a mammogram, these deposits appear as small bright, high-density spots. A 2026 review in the Journal of Breast Imaging from Weill Cornell describes them as "high-density deposits on mammography and echogenic foci on ultrasound, mimicking calcifications." The same review found 19 cases in the published literature and added 3 more.

The research focuses on pigment inside lymph nodes. It does not show that the visible tattoo on your skin routinely blocks or blurs the breast tissue on a mammogram.

Why can pigment in the lymph nodes look like cancer?

Radiologists take calcifications in the armpit lymph nodes seriously. A 2024 case report from Moffitt Cancer Center states that metastatic breast cancer is the most common malignant cause of calcifications in axillary lymph nodes. The same report lists benign causes, including granulomatous disease, fat necrosis, gold deposits in people treated for rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, and tattoo pigment.

Because the two can look alike, pigment deposits sometimes trigger extra imaging. A 2013 BMJ Case Reports article described calcification-like changes in an armpit node that resembled breast cancer and turned out to be tattoo pigment. The authors stressed that recognizing this pattern helps avoid unnecessary invasive tests and surgery.

Pigment can also collect in lymph nodes inside the breast itself. In a 2024 Australian case, a 50-year-old woman with extensive tattoos on her arms, chest wall, and abdomen was recalled after her first screening mammogram for a small calcified breast mass. A biopsy showed an intramammary lymph node full of tattoo pigment.

How common is this problem?

The honest answer is that no one knows the exact rate. The evidence comes from case reports and small case series, not from large studies that followed thousands of tattooed patients. That means it is a recognized pitfall, but the published count of cases is small.

  • The 2026 Journal of Breast Imaging review counted 22 reported cases in total, including its own three.
  • Most published cases involved women having a routine screening mammogram, where the pigment was an incidental finding.
  • The most common sites were axillary lymph nodes and intramammary lymph nodes.

The real number is likely higher than the published count, since radiologists who recognize the pattern rarely write it up. Still, a tattoo does not mean you should expect a callback. For context on how often callbacks happen for any reason, see our mammogram callback guide.

Will I need a biopsy if pigment shows up?

Not always. The 2026 review states that if no other abnormal features are present and the findings can be confidently attributed to tattoo pigment, biopsy is not necessary. If a biopsy is done and confirms pigment, patients can return to their usual screening schedule.

A University of Miami team published a 2022 case series in Breast Disease describing a typical "bubbly" pattern of pseudo-calcifications in the armpit nodes of women with body tattoos. These women had no other abnormal findings on mammogram, ultrasound, or exam. The team proposed an algorithm to reduce lymph node biopsies in this setting, using pathology or long-term stability to confirm the benign cause.

A biopsy is still sometimes the right call. In a 2017 Ochsner case, a 34-year-old woman had ductal carcinoma in situ in the breast and dense spots in her armpit nodes. The node spots looked like spread of cancer, but biopsy showed pigment-laden cells from tattoo ink. Only tissue could tell the two apart. If you want to know more about how calcifications are judged, read our guide to breast calcifications on a mammogram.

What about radiation dots and areola tattoos?

Radiation therapy tattoos. Before external beam radiation, the therapist may tattoo small dots of colored ink on the skin to mark the treatment area. The National Cancer Institute says the dots are about the size of a freckle and stay on the skin for life. We found no published evidence that these tiny marks interfere with later mammograms. Tell the technologist where they are so they are not confused with anything else.

Nipple and areola tattoos. Many people tattoo a nipple and areola after breast reconstruction. This pigment is in the skin, so it behaves like any other tattoo. One 2013 BMJ Case Reports article described changes after nipple-areola reconstruction and tattooing that resembled a recurrence of Paget's disease of the breast. That is a single case, but it shows why your imaging team should know your surgical and tattoo history. Whether you still need mammograms on a reconstructed side depends on your surgery, so ask your care team.

Tattoos directly over the breast. The published cases mostly involved tattoos on the arms, chest wall, and torso, with pigment draining to nearby nodes. A tattoo on the breast skin drains to the same regional nodes, so the same advice applies.

What should I tell the technologist?

A short heads-up at check-in costs nothing and can save a callback. Mention:

  1. Any tattoos on your arms, shoulders, chest, breasts, or upper back, especially large or heavily shaded ones.
  2. Radiation therapy tattoo dots, and roughly where they are.
  3. Nipple or areola tattoos after reconstruction.
  4. Any recent new tattoo near the chest or armpit.

This fits standard mammogram prep. RadiologyInfo.org, run by the American College of Radiology and the Radiological Society of North America, tells patients to share prior surgeries and history with their doctor and to bring prior mammograms. Comparison films matter here: a deposit that has not changed for years is reassuring. The National Cancer Institute also advises avoiding deodorants, powders, and lotions on the day of the exam. RadiologyInfo adds that these products can appear on the mammogram as calcium spots. Our post on why you skip deodorant before a mammogram explains that look-alike.

Tattoos matter for other scans too. The FDA notes rare reports of swelling or burning at tattoo sites during MRI, and advises people to tell the radiologist or technologist rather than skip a needed scan. If a breast MRI is part of your follow-up, mri.md covers what to expect.

The bottom line

Tattoos do not stop a mammogram from working. The main issue is pigment that migrates to lymph nodes and can mimic calcifications, which may lead to a callback or biopsy. The evidence comes from case reports and small series, and radiologists now recognize the pattern. Tell your technologist about your tattoos, bring prior images, and keep up your regular screening.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I avoid getting a tattoo before my mammogram?

There is no published guidance telling people to avoid tattoos before a mammogram. The concern in the research is pigment that has already moved to lymph nodes, which can happen with tattoos of any age. Just tell the technologist about any tattoos, new or old.

Can a tattoo hide breast cancer on a mammogram?

The published case reports describe tattoo pigment creating look-alike findings, not hiding cancers. In one reported case, a woman had both breast cancer and tattoo pigment in her armpit nodes, and biopsy was needed to separate the two. Any new or changing finding still gets a full workup.

Does tattoo pigment in a lymph node mean something is wrong?

No. Pigment in a lymph node is a benign finding. Lymph nodes filter particles from the skin, and tattoo ink is one of the particles they collect. If a biopsy confirms pigment only, the 2026 Journal of Breast Imaging review says you can return to routine screening.

Will laser tattoo removal change how my mammogram looks?

We did not find published studies on laser removal and mammogram findings. Its effect on breast imaging has not been studied. Mention any tattoo removal history to your technologist so the radiologist has the full picture.

Are radiation tattoo dots a problem for future mammograms?

We found no evidence that these freckle-sized dots interfere with mammograms. The National Cancer Institute says tattooed radiation marks stay on the skin for life. Let the technologist know where they are.
